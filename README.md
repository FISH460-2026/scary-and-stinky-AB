With our data, due to the mortalities observed because of the intense level of pollutants, we have a high polution and low polution group to illustrate the differences between the two.

Figure 1A shows the Final (T2) fluoresence in each of the four treatment groups after the data was standardized. Both pollution treatments have notably lower metabolic rates than the non-polluted groups. The Pollution and Predator Cues had a significantly lower average fluoresence thus a lower metabolic rate than even in the pollution only treatment. Those in the predator and pollution treatment were likely not able to detect the predator cues thus did not raise their metabolic rate. 

Figure 1B shows the Final (T2) fluoresence in each of the four treatment groups after the data was standardized. Both pollution treatments have lower metabolic rates than the non-polluted groups but the pollution only group has significantly lower respiratory rates with the pollution and predator cues treatment having slightly higher values. This may indicate that at lower pollution levels, the crabs may be able to detect any predator cues which would explain the higher metabolic rates.

Figure 2A shows the mean antennae flicking per minute in each of the four treatment groups with the standard deviation at high levels of pollution. The control and predator cues treatments stayed practically the same while pollution was dramatically lower. This is likely due to the fact that at such high levels the antennae can't detect anything other than pollutants so they stop moving them altogether. However, there were still antennae movements in the pollution and predator group mean that they possibly were detecting the predator cues (these were also all new crabs so they could have not adjusted to the treatment).

Figure 2B shows the mean antennae flicking per minute in each of the four treatment groups with the standard deviation at low levels of pollution. The results across the control, predator cues, and pollution were approximately the same but the pollution and predator cues flicking was significantly lower than the rest. This may indicate that the pollution slightly decreased their antennae flicking as they may not have detected the predator. 

## Brief Project Description


Research Question: How do Puget Sound pollutants affect Hemigrapsus oregonensis' ability to detect predators through chemosensing?


Our project intends to observe the shore crab's stress response to olfactory predator cues (green crab) and how those stress responses shift with the pressence of pollutants. 


- Hₒ: The pollution will not affect the stress response of H.oregonesis in the presence of C.meaenas.

- Hₐ: The polluted waters will affect the stress response of H.oregonesis in the presence of C.meaenas. 

  - Sub-alt: H.oregoensis will not smell, or otherwise detect, their invasive predators, C. maenas, as effectively in polluted water.
